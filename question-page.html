How can I start investing with less than $100?

A beginner's guide for women who've never invested before · 6 min read · Updated October 2026

Design note 2 · The headline is the questionIt uses the words beginners type. 13 of 18 real questions collected were some form of "where do I start?", and searches with "as a woman" surfaced a competitor, Female Invest, but never FTI.

The short answer: you don't need a lot of money to start. Tools like ETFs and fractional shares let you buy a small slice of the market, and the most important step is the first one. As Sim says, "I always recommend keeping it simple when you begin."

  1. Check your safety net first
  2. Choose a regulated app that suits small amounts
  3. Buy a broad, low-cost index fund
Design note 3 · Answer firstThe answer and the first three steps fit on one phone screen. Today's pages (including competitors') open with context instead. A short answer up top is also what AI tools tend to quote.

Before you start: is this for you?

This guide is for you if you've been meaning to invest "when you have more money", and that day hasn't come. If you've grown up being told money isn't your area, you're in very good company. Many of the women in the Friends That Invest community started exactly there.

The 5 steps

Design note 4 · Numbered steps, each with its sourceReaders skim, so each step is one card with one action. Every step shows where the advice comes from in FTI's own content, so nothing is invented and readers can go deeper.
1

Check your safety net first

Before investing, pay off high-interest debt like credit cards, and build up some emergency savings. As one Stock Market Tea issue put it, an emergency fund is "there for the moments you never see coming."

Not sure which comes first? Use the free Invest or pay off debt flowchart.

From: podcast How to Invest Even When You're Broke (Sept 2024) · Stock Market Tea, 10 Aug 2026

2

Choose a regulated app that suits small amounts

Look for a platform that offers fractional shares, so you can buy part of a share instead of a whole one, and check that it's regulated in your country. Fees matter too, especially when you're investing small amounts.

From: podcast How to Invest Even When You're Broke · Investing Masterclass, Module 8: Choosing an Investing Broker and Fees 101

Design note 5 · To confirm with SimFTI teaches how to choose a broker and compare fees in the Masterclass, but that content isn't public. Her exact criteria and a worked fee example would replace the general wording here.
3

Buy a broad, low-cost index fund

Instead of picking individual companies, an index fund or ETF lets you own a small piece of hundreds of companies at once. This is called passive investing.

🎧 Index Funds Are Changing: Here's What You Need to Know · Why I'm Not Selling My S&P500 Funds

From: podcast episodes above (June and August 2026) · Stock Market Tea explainer "Passive Investing", 8 June 2026

4

Set it to repeat automatically

Choose an amount you're comfortable with and schedule it for the same day every month, ideally just after payday. Investing a fixed amount regularly is called dollar cost averaging, and it means you don't have to guess the "right" day. It's how Sim invests herself: "my investing strategy is on auto-invest… I also dollar cost average instead of trying to time the market."

From: Sim's interview with Sorted

5

Leave it alone

Markets go up and down. The hardest part is not reacting. Talking about KiwiSaver, Sim put it simply: "you shouldn't be pulling your money out of KiwiSaver when the market drops, because it actually solidifies your losses."
